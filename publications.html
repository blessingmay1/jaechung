Publications & research record.

Jaeyeon (Jae) Chung · Rice University · Last updated September 2026
Download Full CV ↓

My work examines how consumers form judgments, trust, and preferences in technology-mediated marketplaces. Recent papers address generative AI and creativity, misinformation and personalized recommendation systems, social media influence, NFT valuation, virtual reality, and prediction markets. Articles appear in the Journal of Marketing, Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Marketing Research, Nature Human Behaviour, and the Harvard Business Review.

Peer-reviewed journal articles

  1. Conditionally accepted, Journal of Marketing
    Chung, J.*, Kim, S.*, & Johar, G. (forthcoming). How personalized video recommendation algorithms induce consumers to believe any misguided information on the platform. Journal of Marketing. (*equal contribution)
    Reliance on personalized recommendation systems on video-sharing platforms raises trust in the platform itself, which in turn increases belief in information encountered through it—including misguided claims. The paper identifies a mechanism by which recommendation algorithms can inadvertently amplify misinformation.
  2. Chung, J., & Park, E. (2026). How social cues drive popularity in NFT art markets. Journal of Marketing.
    doi.org/10.1177/00222429261460019
    In NFT markets where aesthetic quality is difficult to evaluate, visible social cues—prior attention, engagement, and ownership signals—displace aesthetic judgment as the dominant driver of popularity and price. The paper combines OpenSea marketplace data with controlled experiments to document the displacement.
  3. Lee, B.*, & Chung, J.* (2025). Reply to: ChatGPT decreases idea diversity in brainstorming. Nature Human Behaviour. (*equal contribution)
    doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02195-5
    An engaged scholarly exchange on what creativity actually means. We argue that idea diversity and creative-thinking output are two distinct constructs: ChatGPT may reduce the raw count of unique ideas in brainstorming, but it can simultaneously enhance the coherence and usability of the ideas that remain—which is a different, and arguably more important, measure of creative capacity.
  4. Lee, B.*, & Chung, J.* (2024). An empirical investigation of the impact of ChatGPT on creativity. Nature Human Behaviour. (*equal contribution)
    doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01953-1 · Covered by Harvard Business Review, Fast Company, The Conversation (lead article), and 55+ outlets
    A large-scale empirical study showing that ChatGPT improves creative output on everyday tasks not by generating more ideas, but by helping people develop ideas with greater coherence and articulation. The paper distinguishes between ideation and refinement in human–AI collaboration.
  5. Chung, J., Ding, Y., & Kalra, A. (2023). I really know you: How influencers can increase audience engagement by referencing their close social ties. Journal of Consumer Research, 50(4), 683–703.
    Editor's Choice · doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucad019 · Harvard Business Review
    When Instagram influencers reference their close personal relationships rather than remain purely professional, audience engagement rises substantially. Field and experimental studies trace the effect to perceived authenticity through self-disclosure and identify boundary conditions relevant to brand collaborations.
  6. Chung, J., Lee, L., Lehmann, D. R., & Tsai, C. I. (2023). Spending windfall ("found") time on hedonic versus utilitarian activities. Journal of Consumer Research, 49(6), 1118–1139.
    doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac032
    When consumers unexpectedly gain free time—a canceled meeting, an early-ending flight, a snow day—they systematically allocate it toward hedonic rather than utilitarian activities. The paper introduces the concept of "found time" and shows the hedonic shift across field and laboratory studies.
  7. Chung, J., Johar, G. V., Li, Y., Netzer, O., & Pearson, M. (2022). Mining consumer minds: Downstream consequences of host motivations for home-sharing platforms. Journal of Consumer Research, 48(5), 817–838.
    doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab034
    Text mining of Airbnb host descriptions, combined with longitudinal performance data, shows that intrinsic versus monetary motivations predict a cascade of downstream outcomes—guest ratings, retention, pricing behavior, and customer lifetime value—with implications for incentive design on sharing-economy platforms.
  8. Chung, J., & Lee, L. (2019). To buy or to resist: When upward social comparison discourages new product adoption. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research.
    journals.uchicago.edu
    Contrary to the dominant view that envy triggers imitative adoption, upward social comparison can suppress adoption of new products when comparison targets feel too distant or the product threatens the consumer's self-concept.
  9. Chung, J., & Johar, G. V. (2018). The seesaw self: Possessions, identity (de)activation, and task performance. Journal of Marketing Research, 55(5), 752–765.
    journals.sagepub.com
    Possessions activate the identity they represent while suppressing competing aspects of the self. The resulting "seesaw" effect enhances performance on identity-congruent tasks and impairs performance on unrelated ones, with implications for product design and brand positioning.

Working papers & submissions

  1. Submitted after minor revision, Journal of Consumer Psychology
    Jun, Y., Chung, J., & Johar, G. The backfiring effect of virtual reality for high sensation seeking consumers.
    Across six studies—including two field studies (a VR theme park and the National Museum of Korea) and a skin-conductance-response laboratory study—immersive VR substitutes for physical consumption primarily through need fulfillment rather than sensory habituation. The substitution effect is strongest among high-need consumers and is eliminated when VR is framed as a teaser for a subsequent real-world experience.

Work in progress

  1. Under review, Journal of Marketing Research
    Chung, J., & Lee, B. Upward vs. downward trends: Asymmetry in cascade following behavior at Polymarket.
    Using 3,564 Polymarket prediction contracts and controlled experiments, this paper documents a directional asymmetry in herding: consumers overwhelmingly follow upward momentum—even on weak signals—producing significant monetary losses, while downward momentum is largely ignored. The asymmetry is traced to ease of mental simulation, ruling out FOMO, positivity bias, and in-group conformity.
  2. Data analysis in progress
    Chung, J., & Jun, Y. Deception without deceivers.
    Why does misleading content thrive online when much of it originates from creators who never set out to deceive? Under algorithmic pressure to maximize fluency and engagement, well-intentioned creators strip nuance and produce visually decontextualized content that lowers viewers' epistemic vigilance. The design pairs multimodal analysis of YouTube health and finance videos—quantifying visual variance, audio–visual sentiment dissonance, and context loss—with a controlled experiment isolating the role of the visual layer.
  3. Three studies completed
    Schakols, F., Chung, J., & Hofstetter, R. Fostering identity expansion: How AI-collaborative thinking styles drive adoption among managers.
    An identity-based framework for managerial resistance to AI in creative work. Using AI for broad, exploratory thinking—rather than narrow, in-depth analysis—triggers a positive identity transformation, enhancing managers' self-perceptions as creative professionals and significantly increasing adoption intentions across three studies with working managers.
